Teahouse and Spirit World

A game in development, built as an experiment in how meaning is made when resources are not the limit.

Games normally use scarcity to produce their story: you lack something, you obtain it, that acquisition is the plot. This one removes the scarcity and asks what is left. The player can build as much as they want — so the question becomes what makes any of it worth building.

The premise

You work at a teahouse on an island in the sea. Clients arrive by boat, drink their tea, and leave. One day the tea master tells you that a special customer is waiting, and takes you out to an abandoned red building standing in the water — a tunnel running through it, trees growing on its roof. Passing through that tunnel by boat is the crossing into a spirit world.

There, spirits have lost their homes to abandonment, to storm, to forgetting. You are given the power to create anything, and you build homes for them. The spirits never hand out quests. They are mostly nature itself: they react. Places are gods.

Tea is the thesis at its smallest scale. A cup is made for one specific person, cannot be stored or repeated, and is judged as a meeting rather than as an object. A room asks the same question architecturally, a building gives it duration, and a place lets many such meetings accumulate. Cup, room, building, place — the same act, widening.

What the project investigates

The stated purpose is to make experiments that inform how architecture is shaped inside interactive systems — specifically the systems in which a user is free to create whatever they want. This matters for a future in which AI and robotics produce enough growth that resources stop being the constraint, and it matters right now for every tool that puts design into the hands of people who are not architects.

The building system, and the finding I was not looking for

The game needed a construction system, so I built one: layers, a plan and a section read side by side, then docks, stairs, roofs, cornices, ladders, lamps. Testing it produced something I had not set out to research. The rules governing how blocks are formed and placed reward particular attentions and particular ideas very strongly, and suppress others just as strongly. The constraint does not merely permit the design — it does a large share of the designing.

That was not the original research intention. It has become part of what the project investigates, and it is the most directly transferable result so far: any interface that lets a non-architect shape space is making the same kind of decision, usually without noticing.

Method: collage as an honesty meter

The world is assembled by collage, from places I actually know rather than from a borrowed style: the transformed factory in a Danish forest where I grew up, Granada, the Spain–Portugal border, Japanese bathhouses. Each element is rendered at the level of detail I honestly understand it at. What I understand well is built precisely; what I do not stays sketched and open.

This is a working rule, not an aesthetic preference. The mismatch between levels of explanation is what produces the strangeness, and a gap left open is worth more than a bad explanation filling it.

Status

In active development, and early. The construction system, the layered building views and the first version of the world are running; the spirits, the crossing and the narrative rhythm are not yet built.
