Mission

What Marius Vision is, stated as directly as I can.

Marius Vision is a lifework: research into what builds meaning — which places, narratives and actions make a life feel meaningful. Not as theory read at a desk. Knowing and not doing is not knowing, so the research happens by making things and seeing what answers back.

The focus is one specific question. I believe we are moving toward a world where artificial intelligence, robotics and cheap energy remove most practical scarcity — where the problems that currently organize our lives get solved. What is worth making then, when resources were never the limit? My goal is not to save the world; I expect that project to be crowded. It is to investigate how meaning is still created in a world like that. Not to find a final answer — to make the question the center of a working life, one precise experiment at a time.

Architecture is one instrument: a building can stage a ritual, hold a contradiction, and move a body through a question before language simplifies it. Essays and image work are others. Game worlds may become the strongest instrument of all, because they join construction, story, atmosphere, action and consequence in one place — and because in a game, abundance is already real. You can build anything. So what do you build?

The current experiment

I am building a game. You work at a teahouse on an island in the sea. Clients arrive by boat, drink their tea, and leave. One day the tea master tells you a special customer is waiting, and takes you out by boat to an abandoned red building standing in the water, with a tunnel running through it and trees growing on its roof. Passing through that tunnel is the crossing into a spirit world.

There, spirits have lost their homes — to abandonment, to storm, to forgetting. You are given the power to create anything, and you build homes for them. But the spirits never assign quests. They mostly are nature itself. They react. Places are gods.

The game tests a principle I can state plainly: meaning appears when something made with care reaches a particular person, spirit or place — and receives an answer. Tea investigates this at the scale of a cup: made for one person, unrepeatable, judged as a meeting rather than an object. A room investigates it architecturally. A building gives it duration. A place lets many such meetings accumulate. Cup, room, building, place — the same act, widening.

The world of the game is built from places I actually know: the transformed factory in a Danish forest where I grew up, Granada, the Spain–Portugal border, Japanese bathhouses. Each is drawn at exactly the level of detail I honestly understand it — collage as an honesty meter. What I don't understand stays sketched, and the gaps are left for imagination rather than filled with a bad explanation.

This is not a clean philosophy, and I am not trying to teach anyone anything. Some of it stays deliberately unexplained — the open center is the point. The projects on this site are the experiments so far.
